What's an ontology?

What's an ontology?

And why would anyone want one for the Trade Marks Manual? A short explainer with things to try along the way.

In one sentence: an ontology is a map of the ideas inside a big body of writing. It records what each idea is called, what kind of thing it is, how the ideas connect, and exactly where the writing says so.

"Ontology" is a fancy word from philosophy for "the things that exist". Say it on-TOL-uh-jee.

A very big rulebook

People who examine trade mark applications, and people who apply, rely on the Trade Marks Manual of Practice and Procedure. It explains how applications are looked at. It is big: 500 pages, split into 2,460 short sections.

Beside it sit two other books: the Trade Marks Act 1995 and the Trade Marks Regulations 1995. There is one important difference. The Act and the Regulations are the law. The Manual is practice: how the office applies the law. It isn't law itself.

One idea, many names

So the first job of the map is simple: for every idea, write down all the names it goes by. Like a jar with several labels stuck on it.

It also writes down the lookalikes: words that sound close but mean something different. That stops the map getting confused.

Join the dots, with a receipt

Now the clever part. Ideas are joined by arrows, and each arrow is a tiny sentence: idea does something to idea.

There's one strict rule. The map only draws an arrow if a real sentence in the Manual or the law says so. That sentence is the arrow's receipt. No receipt, no arrow.

When the rulebook changes

The Manual is updated all the time: more than 2,000 recorded changes since 2021, most of them small, like fixing links.

Each arrow remembers exactly what its receipt said, like a fingerprint. When a sentence changes, only the arrows that rely on it light up for a person to check. Everything else stays put.

Who wrote it?

Building the whole map by hand would take years. So a computer drafted most of it, reading the Manual's own sentences. That's fast and cheap, but a computer can get things wrong.

So every idea and every arrow carries a sticker saying who wrote it, and whether a trade marks expert has checked it.

Thrown outAn expert read it and said no. Kept apart, never mixed back in.

The golden rule: nothing becomes "checked" just because nobody complained about it. Only a person can check it.

9

Who could it help?

Examiners: a research helper

  • Find every relevant section, whatever words it uses.
  • See how ideas connect, even across distant Parts of the Manual.
  • Keep the Manual (practice) and the Act (law) clearly apart.
  • See exactly what a change to the text affects.

It helps them decide. It never decides for them.

A possible next step, not built yet

Applicants: a plain-English guide

  • Ask about requirements in everyday words.
  • Understand what an objection means, and what the Manual says can help.
  • See the application process as a picture: who does what, and when.
  • Always with links to the source, to read for themselves.

It won't predict whether a mark will be accepted, and it isn't legal advice.

Quick check

For the grown-ups

Does it actually help?

The project tested it on 129 questions. Search guided by the map found better sections than a plain word search, especially for questions asked in everyday words. Against modern search that already understands meaning, it was slightly behind at ranking results. Its clearer strengths are showing its working, joining ideas across Parts, keeping practice and law apart, and spotting what a change affects.

AI models wrote most of the test questions and graded the results, and a fresh measurement is due, so treat these as early results from a prototype.

How big is the real map, and what did it cost?

164 ideas, 372 arrows and 11 kinds, over the Manual, the Act and the Regulations. An expert checked 52 of the ideas and 20 of the arrows; a computer drafted the rest. The computer work cost about US$8.40 in AI model calls. The expensive part is expert time to check the map and keep it up to date.

How would you build one?
  1. Keep a faithful, unchanged copy of the source text, with an address for every section.
  2. List the ideas, with every name each goes by and the lookalikes it is not.
  3. Sort the ideas into a small number of kinds.
  4. Join them with named arrows, only where a sentence says so.
  5. Label who wrote what, and never let "not checked" pass as "checked".
  6. Check it automatically, measure it honestly, and have experts review it.
The fancy words

Ontology: the design of the map: which kinds of idea exist and how they may connect. Knowledge graph: the map itself, built to that design. Triple: one arrow-sentence, idea → link → idea. RDF, SKOS, OWL, SHACL: open standards for writing the map down and checking it, so it isn't locked into one company's product.
