What is an ontology, and why build one for the Trade Marks Manual?

The Trade Marks Manual of Practice and Procedure tells examiners and applicants how trade marks applications are examined. This page explains, one step at a time, what changes when the ideas inside it are drawn as a map. Every example comes from a working demonstrator built over the Manual, the Trade Marks Act 1995 and the Trade Marks Regulations 1995, as captured on .

The Manual tells you what the practice is. An ontology is a map of the ideas inside it: what each idea is, every wording used for it, how the ideas affect one another, and the exact sentence that says so. The map helps people decide. It never decides for them.

Words are not ideas

The Manual names one idea in many words, and uses one word for several ideas. A search box cannot tell. An ontology records the difference.

Coloured squares name the idea. Point at or tap one to read its words.
3

So what is an ontology?

A shared, explicit description of a field: the ideas in it, what kind of thing each idea is, and how the ideas relate. Here, each statement is also tied to the text it comes from.

Shared means everyone, and every computer system, uses the same map. Explicit means it is written down precisely instead of living in people's heads. A field here is Australian trade marks examination.

Think of moving to a new city with a thick book of directions: "from the bakery, walk past the bank, turn left at the post office". Everything is in the book, but to see how the school relates to the park you must read and remember many pages. A map shows the same city at a glance, including routes the book never spells out in one place.

The Manual is the book of directions. The ontology is the map. The word comes from philosophy, where it means the study of what exists. Say it on-TOL-uh-jee.

The blueprint and the building

The ontology says which kinds of idea exist and how they may connect. The knowledge graph is the content built to that plan: these ideas, connections, 2,460 passages and 763 provisions.

Open standards

Built on W3C standards: RDF to store statements, SKOS for labels and synonyms, OWL for kinds and their rules, SHACL for automatic quality checks. Not locked into one vendor's product.

What it is not

Not a replacement for the Manual. Not new law or new practice. Not a decision-maker. Not "the AI": it is a checkable map that an AI assistant can use, and that is just as useful with no AI at all.

4

Every idea is a kind of thing

Each idea is sorted by what it is into one of eleven kinds, in three families. The families tell the basic story of examination: the law asks questions, the questions are asked about something, and a process surrounds them.

All ideas

solid reviewed by a trade marks expertdashed machine-written, unreviewed

Kinds say what an idea is, not what it does. Being a factor, an exception or a remedy is not a kind. Those are roles an idea plays towards another idea, so the map records them as connections. A machine judged which kind each idea belongs to, and that judgement is unreviewed.

How the kinds connect

Zoom out and hundreds of connections between ideas become a few patterns between kinds. The clearest one:

What a mark containsits context qualify, may give rise to →the tests state the threshold for →the grounds

No single passage holds this pattern, and rewording a page does not change it. It is the shape of examination practice.

Every connection type says which kinds it may join: performs runs from a process role to a procedural step, never the other way. A connection whose ends are the wrong kinds is caught automatically; that check flagged five connections recorded the wrong way round.

Connections from one family (row) to another (column), with the commonest types.

All the connection types, and how often each is used

Each type has a written definition, an example and a counter-example, and is marked as being about law or about practice. There is no vague "is related to": a passage that only mentions two ideas together is not a connection between them. Pick a type to see every connection that uses it.

When the text changes

The Manual changes all the time. Because every connection stores the address and a fingerprint of the exact text it quotes, the map can say what to re-check when a page is rewritten, and leave everything else alone.

Amendments recorded on the Manual's pages

amendments since 2021, from the notes on each page as captured on (2026 is a part year).

The reasons given

Pick a page: what would a rewrite touch?

Try it: change a quoted sentence

A fingerprint is a short code worked out from the exact text. Change one character and the code changes, so the system knows the connection resting on that text needs a person to look again.

Edit the sentence and watch the fingerprint. The project uses a stronger fingerprint (SHA-256); this page uses a simpler one so it works anywhere.

The levels above the text change much less. An idea survives a rewording, and a kind of idea rests on no single passage. The same works for legislation: ideas, connections and passages record the provisions they rest on, so an amendment to a provision can be traced to everything that relies on it.

8

Who it helps

One map, two doors. For examiners, a research and decision-support tool. For applicants, a way to understand requirements, the process and objections.

The goal: help examiners quickly find the relevant sections of the Manual, the legislation and related material, see how they connect, and reach their own decision faster and more consistently.

  1. Find by meaning, not just matching words. Ask in your own words; the map recognises the ideas behind them, whichever wording the Manual uses.
  2. Join the dots across Parts. One question can pull together the ground (Part 29), the specific practice (Part 32A on plants) and the remedies (conditions of registration, limiting the specification).
  3. See the route, not just the result. The ideas recognised, connections followed and passages found are on screen.
  4. Practice and law kept apart, including inside answers.
  5. A checklist of what bears on a test. Open and see what qualifies it, what may give rise to it and what does not, each with its sentence.
  6. Learning the territory. For a new examiner, the map is a mental model of the whole Manual.
  7. Keeping the Manual consistent. Whoever maintains it can see which passages speak about one idea, and what an amendment touches.

Where following connections helped most

Top-ten quality out of 100: meaning-based search, then with the ontology. Chosen as the clearest gains; across all questions the picture is mixed (stop 9).

What "decision support" means here

It supports: it finds, organises, connects and cites.

It never decides: it never states an examination outcome, never says how confident it is that a ground applies, and never applies the law to an application's facts. Asked to, it declines and explains the general practice. The examiner's judgement and the Registrar's discretion are untouched.

◇A possible next step. The demonstrator was built for examiners. This describes what the same ontology could offer applicants. It is not a service that exists today.

The goal: help people applying for a trade mark understand what the requirements are, how the process works, and what an objection raised during examination means.

  1. Applicants use everyday words. "Famous person", "brand name", "someone has a similar mark". The map's wordings, including everyday phrasings, bridge to the Manual's language.
  2. An objection becomes a small, readable map. For "not capable of distinguishing": the ground, the test, what can help (evidence of use) and where the how-to guide is.
  3. The process becomes a picture. Who acts, what happens next, and which document each step produces.
  4. Answers that show their sources, labelled Manual or Act, so an applicant can read the original.
  5. The same map, a different door. Applicants and examiners would read the same practice, described with the same ideas.

Guardrails that matter even more here

  • It explains what the Manual and the legislation say. It does not predict whether an application will succeed.
  • It is not legal advice.
  • It never presents office practice as a statutory rule.
  • It shows which content an expert reviewed and which a machine drafted.

Possible benefits, expected and not measured: applicants arriving better prepared, fewer avoidable objections, and objections that are easier to understand and answer.

9

What was measured

The project tested whether the ontology improves search, rather than assuming it. The answer depends on what it is compared with.

The test. 129 questions: 119 written by an AI model in four kinds, plus 10 that a trade marks expert reviewed. Three search systems were fixed before any result was graded:

  • Keyword: words only, like a search box.
  • Hybrid: keyword plus matching by meaning. Good modern search, with no ontology.
  • Ontology: hybrid, plus recognising ideas, following their connections and adding their linked passages.

Another AI model graded every passage in each system's top results. Each top ten was scored out of 100 (a standard measure called nDCG@10). The lines show 95% intervals: a line that crosses zero means no clear difference.

Against keyword search

The ontology clearly helped: +7.9 points overall, and +15.1 on everyday-language questions.

Against good modern search

Slightly worse overall, −4.1 points. Very common ideas pulled loosely related passages into the top ten: Registrar is named in more than 400 passages.

The ontology's clearest value

Not "beating the best search engine at ranking". Bridging everyday language, showing its working, connecting Parts, keeping practice and law apart, and impact analysis.

Caveats. AI models wrote most questions and graded the results; no person graded them. Scored against only the passages the expert listed for their 10 questions, with no model judging, the three systems were level. The connections were restructured after this measurement (9 October 2026), and a fresh one is pending. These numbers describe a prototype.

10

Trust, and the limits

For a government audience this matters most: the system always knows, and always shows, what an expert reviewed and what a machine wrote.

Machine · unreviewed

A machine drafted it from the text and no person has checked it. It names the exact model, the date, the passages it rests on and its reasoning. ideas and connections are in this state.

Reviewed

A trade marks expert read it and accepted it on a recorded date. ideas and connections, plus ten test questions and a list of things the system must never do.

Rejected

An expert read it and threw it out. Rejected records are kept apart, never mixed with the others.

A machine-written record never becomes approved by being left alone, however long it goes unchallenged. Only a recorded human decision changes its state.

It will never

  • decide an application, state an examination outcome, or say how likely it is that a ground applies;
  • present the Manual as law, or blur Manual practice into the legislation;
  • present machine-written content as expert-reviewed.

Not finished, or not yet checked

  • Most of it is unreviewed, including every idea's kind and all 129 prepared answers.
  • Some ideas are recorded twice, such as Registrar and Registrar of Trade Marks. Flagged for a person, not merged.
  • Recognising an idea means matching its wordings, so "who has to sign" matches the idea sign.
  • One snapshot. No automatic update loop yet, though every record is anchored so one can be built.
  • Search ranking does not beat good modern search overall (stop 9).

The most useful thing an examiner can do with the map is find what it has wrong.

11

How to build or adopt one

Eleven steps, distilled from what this project did and learned. Machines draft; people judge. Open a step to see how the demonstrator did it.

    Lessons along the way

    • Let machines draft and people judge. A model drafted hundreds of connections for a few dollars; a person's time is best spent judging them.
    • Exact matching first. Anything that can be done by exact matching should be, because it needs no review.
    • Common ideas need care. An idea named everywhere, such as Registrar, can flood search results.
    • A co-mention is not a relationship. Re-checking 226 vague or misfiled links one at a time kept 130, re-labelled with a real meaning, and withdrew 96.
    • Keep the yardstick independent. The expert-reviewed set was frozen so it could fairly measure the machine's work.

    What it cost

    About US$8.40 in AI model calls for the whole demonstrator: drafting, answering 129 test questions and grading search results. The expensive part of an ontology is not computing. It is the expert time to review it and keep it current.
