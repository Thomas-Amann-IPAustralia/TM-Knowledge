# What is an ontology, and why build one for the Trade Marks Manual?

*A grounding document for an explainer video. Plain English, Australian spelling.
Every figure describes a working demonstrator as it stood on 12 October 2026, built
over a copy of the Trade Marks Manual of Practice and Procedure, the Trade Marks Act
1995 and the Trade Marks Regulations 1995 captured on 3 August 2026.*

---

## Read this first: what this document is for

This document is the source for a short explainer video, in the spirit of "explain it
like I'm five". It covers what an ontology is and why an organisation might build or
adopt one. The worked example throughout is the Australian Trade Marks Manual.

**Who the video is for.** People who know trade marks or work near them: trade marks
examiners and their managers, policy and IT colleagues. Secondly, people who apply for
trade marks. Assume the viewer has never heard the word "ontology" and has no
technical background.

**The one message the viewer should leave with:**

> The Manual tells you what the practice is. An ontology is a map of the ideas inside
> it. The map records what each idea is, all the different words used for it, how the
> ideas affect one another, and the exact sentence that says so. With the map, people
> and computers can find, connect and check the guidance far more easily. The map
> helps people decide; it never decides for them.

**Five ground rules that apply to everything in the video** (section 20 has the full
presenter notes):

1. The ontology **supports** a person's judgement. It never makes an examination
   decision and never predicts whether a particular trade mark will be accepted.
2. **The Manual is practice, not law.** The Trade Marks Act 1995 and the Trade Marks
   Regulations 1995 are the law. The ontology keeps the two apart at every step.
3. **Most of the ontology was drafted by a machine** and no trade marks expert has yet
   reviewed it. Every machine-drafted piece is labelled that way, wherever it appears.
4. The demonstrator is a **prototype**. It is not official guidance, not a public
   service and not legal advice.
5. In Australia, **"trade mark" is two words**.

## 1. The short version

Imagine you move to a new city. Someone hands you a thick book of directions: "From
the bakery, walk past the bank and turn left at the post office…" Everything you need
is somewhere in that book. But to work out how the school relates to the park, you
have to read many pages and remember them all.

A **map** shows the same city at a glance. Each place is a dot. The roads between
places are drawn and signposted. You can see routes that the book never spells out in
one place.

**The Trade Marks Manual is the book of directions. An ontology is the map.**

The map has five ingredients:

| On a city map | In the Trade Marks ontology | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Places | **Ideas** (also called concepts) | "evidence of use", "section 41 ground for rejection", "examiner" |
| Place names and nicknames | **Wordings** (also called labels) | "specification of goods and services" is usually just called "specification" |
| Types of place: school, shop, park | **Kinds** of idea | a *ground of refusal*, a *legal test*, a *procedural step*, a *process role* |
| Signposted roads | **Connections**, each with a name that says what it means | "no inherent adaptation to distinguish — *is overcome by* → evidence of use" |
| A note saying which survey drew each road | **Evidence**: every connection is pinned to the exact sentence it comes from | the Manual, Part 22, or the Act, section 41 |

There is a sixth ingredient that matters most to a government audience: **a label on
every item saying who wrote it.** Either a trade marks expert reviewed it, or a
machine drafted it and nobody has checked it yet.

## 2. The starting point: guidance written as text

The **Trade Marks Manual of Practice and Procedure** is IP Australia's published
statement of how trade marks applications are examined. In the copy the demonstrator
uses:

- **500 pages in 54 Parts**, broken into **2,460 passages**. A passage is a few
  paragraphs under a single heading.
- Parts range from filing requirements and fees, through the grounds for rejecting an
  application (for example Part 22, "Section 41 – Capable of Distinguishing", and Part
  29, "Section 43 – Trade Marks likely to Deceive or Cause Confusion"), to
  oppositions, hearings, renewals and the Madrid Protocol. The largest, Part 14 on
  classifying goods and services, holds 743 passages.

Beside the Manual sit the two pieces of law it explains:

- the **Trade Marks Act 1995**, with 316 provisions; and
- the **Trade Marks Regulations 1995**, with 447 provisions.

The Manual refers to the legislation constantly. Of 2,687 references from the Manual
to a provision, **97%** could be matched automatically to the exact provision.

**Why the difference between practice and law matters.** The Manual records the
Registrar's examination practice. It does not bind the Registrar's discretion and it
is not legislation. A system that blurs the Manual into the Act would be misleading.
This demonstrator gives each source its own colour and mark wherever it appears, in
diagrams and in answers: a blue square for the Manual, an amber diamond for the Act
and a plum hexagon for the Regulations.

## 3. Why text alone is hard to use

A search box sees only one thing: **words in passages.** It finds a passage when the
words you type appear in it. Four things make that harder than it sounds.

### 3.1 One idea, many wordings

The Manual often names the same idea in different words. A search for the formal term
misses every passage that uses another wording.

| The idea | Passages using the formal term | Passages naming the idea in any of its wordings | Missed by searching the formal term |
|---|---|---|---|
| **specification of goods and services** | 3 | 109, in five wordings ("specification" alone in 104) | 106 |
| **plant variety name** | 4 | 17, in six wordings ("variety name", "variety denomination", "name of a plant variety"…) | 13 |
| **ordinary consumer** | 6 | 19, in four wordings ("average consumer", "ordinary person", "reasonable consumer") | 13 |
| **surrounding circumstances** | 6 | 15, in three wordings ("relevant circumstances"…) | 9 |

The ontology records the wordings once, on the idea. Every passage using any of them
is then linked to the same idea, whatever words it uses.

People outside the office use different words again. An applicant might say "a famous
person" where the Manual says "well-known person", or "brand name" where the Act says
"trade mark". The ontology can hold everyday phrasings too. In the demonstrator a
machine wrote some for search, and they are labelled as machine-written.

### 3.2 One word, several ideas

Words are slippery in the other direction too.

- **"Consent"** appears in 54 passages. It can mean a well-known person's written
  permission to use their name (section 43), a letter of consent from another trade
  mark owner (section 44), the consent of the ACCC, or a Minister's consent under
  another Act. The ontology records that "consent" on its own is too general to count
  as any one of those ideas.
- **"Sign"** is a legal term. The Act says *"A trade mark is a sign used, or intended
  to be used, to distinguish goods or services…"* (section 17), and that a sign
  *"includes … any letter, word, name, signature, numeral, device, brand, heading,
  label, ticket, aspect of packaging, shape, colour, sound or scent"* (section 6). In
  "who has to *sign* the declaration?" it is an ordinary verb. The demonstrator really
  did confuse the two, and section 14 lists this as a known limit.

To help with this, every idea in the ontology can carry **"not" labels**: wordings
that look close but mean something else. For example, *connotation* is recorded as
**not** the same as *denotation*, *deceptively similar* or *descriptive meaning*.

### 3.3 Related guidance is spread across the Manual

One idea can matter in many Parts. **Connotation** (a secondary or implied meaning in a
trade mark) is named in 44 passages, in Parts including 19A, 20, 22, 29 and 32A.
**Evidence of use** is named in 54 passages, in Parts including 14, 21, 22, 23 and 32A. A
reader following one Part sees one piece at a time. A map shows every piece together.

### 3.4 The text keeps changing

The Manual's own page histories record **2,039 amendments since 2021**: 555 in 2021, 784
in 2022, 80 in 2023, 161 in 2024, 346 in 2025 and 113 so far in 2026. The most common
reasons given are "Update hyperlinks" (741), no reason at all (467), "Page renamed.
Links updated." (128) and "Terminology updated to reflect legislative changes" (45).
Anything built on the text has to cope with change. Section 9 explains how the
ontology does.

## 4. So what is an ontology?

**An ontology is a shared, explicit description of a field: the ideas in it, what kind
of thing each idea is, and how the ideas relate.** In this project each statement is
also tied to the text it comes from.

- **Shared**: everyone, and every computer system, uses the same map.
- **Explicit**: written down in a precise, structured way, not left in people's heads.
- **A field**: here, Australian trade marks examination.

The word comes from philosophy, where "ontology" is the study of what exists. In
information science it simply means "a careful list of the things that exist in a
field and how they connect". Pronounce it *on-TOL-uh-jee*.

### 4.1 Building block 1: ideas

An **idea** (or **concept**) is one thing people reason about, whatever words they use
for it. The demonstrator holds **164 ideas**, for example:

- grounds and tests: *section 41 ground for rejection*, *capable of distinguishing*,
  *likely to deceive or cause confusion*, *deceptively similar*, *connotation*;
- what a trade mark contains: *plant variety name*, *geographical reference*, *image
  of a person*, *internet domain name*;
- context: *priority date*, *reputation*, *ordinary consumer*, *well-known person*;
- the process: *applicant*, *examiner*, *Registrar*, *examination*, *acceptance*,
  *opposition*, *adverse report*, *evidence of use*, *condition of registration*,
  *Register of Trade Marks*.

A trade marks expert reviewed **52** of the 164. A machine drafted the other **112**
from the Manual's text, and they are labelled unreviewed.

### 4.2 Building block 2: wordings

Each idea has one **preferred label** and any number of **alternative labels**, plus
the "not" labels described in section 3.2. For example, *section 43 ground for
rejection* is also written "s 43 objection", "section 43 objection" and "ground for
rejection under section 43", and it is recorded as **not** a *section 41 ground for
rejection* or a *section 44 ground*.

By matching every idea's wordings against the text, with no AI involved, the
demonstrator links ideas to passages **7,128 times**, across **1,649 of the 2,460
passages**.

### 4.3 Building block 3: kinds

Every idea is sorted by **what it is** into one of **eleven kinds**, grouped into three
families. The families tell the basic story of examination: *the law asks questions,
the questions are asked about something, and a process surrounds them.*

**Family 1 — the questions the law asks**

| Kind | In plain words | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Ground of refusal | a reason an application can be refused | section 41 ground for rejection; section 43 ground for rejection; conflict with other signs; scandalous matter |
| Legal test | a question the decision maker has to answer | capable of distinguishing; likely to deceive or cause confusion; deceptively similar; connotation |
| Principle | a settled rule about how those questions are answered: where the onus lies, whether there is a discretion | presumption of registrability; mandatory application of the section; office practice |

**Family 2 — what those questions are asked about**

| Kind | In plain words | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Subject matter | the thing being examined | trade mark; sign; certification trade mark; shape trade mark; series of trade marks |
| Sign content | something a trade mark contains or conveys | plant variety name; geographical reference; image of a person; INN stem (part of a medicine's international name) |
| Context | something outside the trade mark that the assessment looks at | priority date; reputation; ordinary consumer; relevant market; well-known person |
| Use in trade | something a person does with a trade mark | use of a trade mark; honest concurrent use; prior use |

**Family 3 — the process around them**

| Kind | In plain words | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Process role | who acts in the process | applicant; examiner; Registrar; opponent; Hearing Officer; trade mark attorney |
| Procedural step | an event that moves an application or registration along | examination; acceptance; opposition; hearing; extension of time; renewal of registration |
| Instrument or record | a document or entry the process produces or receives | trade mark application; specification of goods and services; adverse report; evidence of use; endorsement; condition of registration; Register of Trade Marks |
| External instrument | a treaty or law outside the Trade Marks Act that practice refers to | Madrid Protocol; classification of goods and services; repealed Act |

**Kinds say what an idea *is*, not what it *does*.** Being a "factor", an "exception" or
a "remedy" is not a kind. Those are roles an idea plays *towards another idea*, so the
ontology records them as connections, described next. A machine judged which kind each
idea belongs to, and that judgement is unreviewed.

### 4.4 Building block 4: connections

A **connection** says how one idea bears on another. It is written as a tiny sentence
in three parts, *subject — connection → object*. Computer scientists call this a
**triple**.

> **plant variety name** — *may give rise to* → **section 43 ground for rejection**
>
> **no inherent adaptation to distinguish** — *is overcome by* → **evidence of use**
>
> **image of a person** — *does not give rise to* → **section 43 ground for rejection**
>
> **examiner** — *performs* → **examination**
>
> **examination** — *precedes* → **acceptance**

The demonstrator holds **372 connections**. A trade marks expert reviewed **20** of them.
A machine drafted the other **352**, and they are labelled unreviewed.

Four rules make these connections useful rather than decorative.

1. **Every connection type has a name and a meaning.** There are about fifty, such as
   *qualifies*, *may give rise to*, *does not give rise to*, *is overcome by*, *states
   threshold for*, *performs*, *files*, *issues*, *results in*, *is recorded in*,
   *precedes* and *holds delegation from*. Each has a written definition, an example
   and a counter-example. Each is marked as being about **law** or about **practice**.
2. **There is no vague "is related to".** If a passage only *mentions* two ideas
   together, that is not a connection between them. The project tried a catch-all
   "related" link and then retired it, because it told the reader nothing. Re-checking
   226 vague or misfiled links one at a time kept 130, re-labelled with a real
   meaning, and withdrew 96.
3. **Every connection type says which kinds it may join.** *Performs* runs from a
   process role to a procedural step, not the other way round. A connection whose ends
   are the wrong kinds is caught automatically: the check flagged five connections
   that had been recorded the wrong way round.
4. **Every connection is pinned to its evidence.** It records the exact sentence it
   rests on, word for word, with the address of that passage and a fingerprint of the
   text (see 4.5).

**Zoom out**, and the hundreds of connections between ideas turn into a few patterns
between kinds. The clearest:

> *What a trade mark contains, and the context it is used in, bear on the legal tests.
> The tests decide the grounds of refusal.*

For example, 79 connections run from family 2 (what the questions are asked about) to
family 1 (the questions themselves). Of those, 40 *qualify* a test or ground, 32 *may
give rise to* one and 5 *do not give rise to* one. Within the process family, roles
*perform* steps, steps *result in* records, and the Registrar *keeps* the Register. That pattern is the shape of examination practice. No single passage holds
it, and rewording a page does not change it.

### 4.5 Building block 5: evidence, or "receipts"

Every statement the ontology makes carries a receipt:

- **where**: a permanent address for the passage of the Manual, or the provision of
  the Act or Regulations;
- **what**: the exact words quoted, checked by a computer letter for letter against
  the source;
- **a fingerprint**: a short code computed from the exact text. If the text changes,
  the fingerprint stops matching, so the system knows that statement needs
  re-checking.

So nothing in the ontology is "just known". If the Manual or the legislation does not
support a statement, the statement is not written.

### 4.6 Building block 6: who wrote it

Every record is in one of three states, and they are never mixed up:

- **Machine-written, unreviewed**: a machine drafted it from the text, and no person
  has checked it. It is labelled "Machine · unreviewed" everywhere it appears, and it
  names the exact model that wrote it, the date, the passages it rests on and its
  reasoning.
- **Reviewed**: a trade marks expert read it and accepted it on a recorded date.
- **Rejected**: an expert read it and threw it out.

A machine-written record **never becomes "approved" by being left alone**, however long
it goes unchallenged. Only a recorded human decision changes its state.

### 4.7 Ontology, knowledge graph, taxonomy: what is the difference?

Think of a ladder, where each rung adds something:

1. **An index** lists words and the pages they appear on.
2. **A glossary** explains what each word means.
3. **A thesaurus** adds synonyms and "see also" links.
4. **A taxonomy** sorts things into a family tree: "a shape trade mark *is a kind of*
   non-traditional sign".
5. **An ontology** adds kinds, named connections of many types (not only "is a kind
   of"), and rules about what may connect to what.

**The ontology is the blueprint; the knowledge graph is the building.** The ontology
says which kinds of idea exist and how they may connect. The **knowledge graph** is the
actual content: these 164 ideas, these 372 connections, these 2,460 passages, these
763 provisions.

The demonstrator uses open international standards from the W3C, the body behind web
standards: **RDF** to store the triples, **SKOS** for labels and synonyms, **OWL** for
the kinds and their rules, and **SHACL** for automatic quality checks. Because the
standards are open, the work is not locked into one vendor's product.

### 4.8 What an ontology is not

- **Not a replacement for the Manual.** It points into the Manual and the legislation;
  they remain the authority.
- **Not new law or new practice.** It describes what the text says, with receipts.
- **Not a decision-maker.** It organises and finds; people judge.
- **Not "the AI".** It is a structured, checkable map. An AI assistant can use the map
  to find better evidence and show its working (section 8). The map is just as useful
  with no AI at all: in search, in training, in a diagram on a wall.

## 5. Worked example A: a well-known person's name

**The situation.** An application is for a trade mark that includes the name or image
of a well-known person. What does the guidance say? This sits mainly in Part 29 of the
Manual (section 43, trade marks likely to deceive or cause confusion), with related
material in other Parts.

**The little map the ontology draws** (all connections below are machine-written and
unreviewed; each quote is the Manual's own words):

```
            image of a person ──does not give rise to──▶ ┐
   long-deceased person ──does not give rise to──▶       │
                                                         ▼
 well-known person ──may give rise to──▶  SECTION 43 GROUND FOR REJECTION
                                                         │
                                                  is overcome by
                                                         ▼
                                              written permission ──is recorded in──▶ endorsement
```

- **image of a person — *does not give rise to* → section 43 ground for rejection.**
  The Manual: *"A section 43 ground for rejection should not be raised merely because a
  trade mark contains the image of a person."*
- **section 43 ground for rejection — *is overcome by* → written permission.**
  The Manual: *"An applicant may overcome a section 43 ground for rejection in relation
  to a name by providing the well-known person's written permission to use their name
  as a trade mark."*
- **written permission — *is recorded in* → endorsement.**
  The Manual: *"…Where permission is provided, this should be made clear by way of an
  endorsement."*
- **deceased person — *does not give rise to* → section 43 ground for rejection.**
  The Manual: *"Generally, a section 43 ground for rejection will not arise in respect
  of long deceased historical figures, even if they were well known for the same field
  as the specified goods/services are in."*

**Why this helps.** In five arrows the map shows when the ground may arise, when it
does not, how it can be overcome and what record that leaves. Each arrow opens onto
the sentence that supports it. A search box would return a list of passages and leave
the reader to assemble that picture themselves.

**The law, kept apart.** The legal test comes from the Act, section 43. The detail
about well-known persons, permission and endorsements is Manual practice, and the map
and answers show it as such.

**The "consent" trap.** The Manual often says "consent", but not every "consent" is
this idea (section 3.2). A letter of consent under section 44 is a different thing
altogether, and the ontology records that difference.

## 6. Worked example B: an objection that a mark is "not capable of distinguishing"

**The situation.** A common objection is that a trade mark is not capable of
distinguishing the applicant's goods or services from other people's (section 41 of
the Act). What does that mean, and what can be done about it?

**The map:**

```
 capable of distinguishing ──states threshold for──▶ SECTION 41 GROUND FOR REJECTION
          ▲
     establishes
          │
   evidence of use ◀──is overcome by── no inherent adaptation to distinguish
```

- **capable of distinguishing — *states threshold for* → section 41 ground for
  rejection.** This rests on **the Act** itself, section 41: *"An application for the
  registration of a trade mark must be rejected if the trade mark is not capable of
  distinguishing the applicant's goods or services in respect of which the trade mark
  is sought to be registered (the designated goods or services) from the goods or
  services of other persons."*
- **no inherent adaptation to distinguish — *is overcome by* → evidence of use.**
  The Manual (Part 22): *"In some cases it may be considered that the trade mark has no
  inherent adaptation to distinguish and the applicant would therefore need to show
  sufficient evidence that the trade mark did distinguish the applicant's goods or
  services, at the date of filing."*
- **evidence of use — *establishes* → capable of distinguishing.**
  The Manual (Part 10) describes a trade mark *"lacking in inherent adaptation to
  distinguish"* being *"shown through evidence of use to be capable of distinguishing"*.

The Manual also has a whole Part on overcoming this ground (Part 23, "Overcoming
Grounds for Rejection under Section 41 – including Evidence of Use"), including an
annex on *how to supply evidence of use*. The map links the objection, the test, the
remedy and the how-to guide, even though they sit in different Parts.

**Why this helps.** An examiner can see the whole route from the Act's test to the
Manual's practice on evidence. An applicant can understand what an objection is
asking of them. Neither is told whether *their* evidence will be enough. That remains
a judgement for the examiner.

## 7. Worked example C: the process, who does what and in what order

The ontology is not only about legal tests. It also maps the process, with roles,
steps and records connected by meaningful verbs (all machine-written, unreviewed):

- **examiner — *performs* → examination**
- **examination — *operates on* → trade mark application**
- **examination — *precedes* → acceptance.** The Manual: *"An application must be
  accepted if, after examination, it is found to have been made in accordance with the
  provisions of the Act … and there are no grounds for rejecting it."*
- **deferment of acceptance — *prevents* → acceptance**
- **opposition — *is commenced by* → notice of opposition**
- **Hearing Officer — *performs* → hearing**
- **delegate — *holds delegation from* → Registrar**, resting on the Act, section 206:
  *"The Registrar may by signed instrument delegate all or any of his or her powers or
  functions to a prescribed employee, or employees in a prescribed class."*
- **Registrar — *keeps* → Register of Trade Marks**, resting on the Act, section 207.

**Why this helps.** A newcomer, whether a new examiner or a first-time applicant, can
see the process as a picture: who acts, what happens next, and what document each step
produces.

## 8. Worked example D: "Ask the Manual"

The demonstrator includes a question-and-answer tool called **Ask the Manual**. It
works in four visible steps:

1. **Recognise.** It matches the question's words against every idea's wordings,
   including everyday phrasings.
2. **Follow.** It follows the connections from those ideas to related ideas.
3. **Gather and rank.** It collects the passages that name those ideas or cite their
   provisions, and blends them with an ordinary keyword search.
4. **Answer.** An AI language model writes an answer **only from those passages**,
   citing each claim. A computer then checks every quote word for word against the
   source and drops any it cannot find. The answer labels what is Manual practice and
   what is the Act or the Regulations. It **declines to say how any particular
   application would be decided.**

The screen shows all of this: the ideas recognised, the path followed and the passages
found, with plain keyword search beside it for comparison. The working is visible,
not hidden.

**Example 1 — a question in an applicant's own words:** *"Does using a well-known
person's name in my trade mark raise a refusal if my goods are in their field?"*

- **Recognised:** *well-known person*; *trade mark*.
- **Followed:** *well-known person — may give rise to → section 43 ground for
  rejection*; *well-known person — qualifies → deception*, *confusion* and *implied
  endorsement*.
- **The answer opened with:** *"Potentially, but not automatically merely because your
  goods are in that person's field. I cannot determine the outcome of your particular
  application."* It then set out the **Act's** test (section 43) separately from the
  **Manual's** practice: how well known the person is, whether consumers would identify
  the name with that person, how closely the goods relate to their field, and the role
  of permission.
- **Search quality on this question** (out of 100, see section 13): keyword search 84,
  modern meaning-based search 87, with the ontology 95.

**Example 2 — an everyday question:** *"Can a rose variety name also be my trade
mark?"*

- **Recognised:** *plant variety name*, from the words "variety name".
- **Followed:** *plant variety name — may give rise to → section 43 ground for
  rejection.* The Manual (Part 29): *"If a trade mark contains or consists of word/s
  which connote a particular plant variety or other plant name, and is applied to
  plants or plant materials of the same or a closely related genus, it is likely to
  deceive or cause confusion as to the true nature of those plants or plant materials
  and a ground for rejection under section 43 of the Act will be appropriate."*
- **What it found:** the ontology-guided top ten held **five relevant passages**
  (graded 2 or 3 out of 3 by a grading model) that were in neither keyword search's top
  ten nor the top ten of a modern meaning-based search. They include material in Part 32A,
  "Examination of Trade Marks for Plants".
- **The answer** explained two separate issues, distinctiveness (Act, section 41) and
  deception or confusion (Act, section 43), and that the Manual says a letter of
  consent from a Plant Breeder's Rights holder does not overcome them. It ended:
  *"They do not establish that any particular rose variety name would qualify."*
- **Search quality on this question:** keyword search 75, meaning-based search 85,
  with the ontology 85. It tied with good modern search on the score, but found
  different relevant passages.

**Across all 129 prepared answers:** every answer carries at least one citation that a
computer located word for word in the source, with 4.7 on average. 86 of the 129 cite
the Act or the Regulations as well as the Manual. 16 declined the part of the question
that asked how an application would be decided. All 129 are machine-written and
unreviewed.

## 9. Worked example E: when the Manual changes

Because every connection stores the address and fingerprint of the exact text it
quotes, the system can answer a question that is very hard to answer from text alone:

> **"If this page is rewritten, what else needs to be checked?"**

Pick any page of the Manual, and the demonstrator lists exactly which connections rest
on its passages. Those are the ones to re-check. Everything else is left alone. Today
**162 of the 500 pages** hold a passage that some connection quotes.

The higher levels of the map change much less than the text:

- the **wording** of a passage changes often: most of the 2,039 amendments were links,
  renames and wording;
- an **idea** survives a rewording;
- a **kind** of idea rests on no single passage at all.

The same idea extends to legislation. Ideas, connections and passages all record the
provisions they rest on, so an amendment to a provision can be traced to everything
that relies on it. This is called **impact analysis**, and it is something a search
box cannot do.

## 10. Use case 1: examiners, a research and decision-support tool

**The goal:** help examiners quickly find the relevant sections of the Manual, the
legislation and related material, see how they connect, and reach their *own*
decision faster and more consistently.

**What the ontology gives an examiner:**

1. **Find by meaning, not just by matching words.** Ask in your own words; the
   ontology recognises the ideas behind them, whichever wording the Manual uses.
2. **Join the dots across Parts.** One question can pull together the ground (for
   example Part 29), the specific practice (for example Part 32A on plants) and the
   remedies (for example conditions of registration, or limiting the specification).
3. **See the route, not just the result.** The ideas recognised, the connections
   followed and the passages found are all on screen. That is the difference between
   a black box and a research assistant.
4. **Practice and law, clearly separated.** Every passage is marked as Manual (blue
   square), Act (amber diamond) or Regulations (plum hexagon), including inside
   answers.
5. **A checklist of what bears on a test.** Open an idea such as *likely to deceive or
   cause confusion* and see what qualifies it, what may give rise to it and what does
   not, each with its sentence.
6. **Learning the territory.** For a new examiner the map is a mental model of the
   whole Manual: kinds of idea, how they connect, and where to read more. The
   demonstrator includes a seven-step guided tour.
7. **Keeping the Manual consistent.** For whoever maintains the Manual, the map shows
   which passages speak about the same idea, and impact analysis (section 9) shows
   what an amendment touches.

**Illustrative scenarios** (built from the demonstrator's real questions):

- *"When two series marks use the same common surname and initial, is that usually the
  part people focus on to compare them?"* This question crosses Parts, and it is the
  one where the ontology helped most: top-ten quality rose from 65 with meaning-based
  search to 91 with the ontology.
- *"What do I do if the goods and services wording in my international trade mark
  application does not fit the usual classification terms?"* 76 rose to 93.
- *"How long do I have to notify a provisional refusal if it is based on an
  opposition?"* 69 rose to 86.

**What "decision support" means here, and what it does not:**

- It **supports**: it finds, organises, connects and cites.
- It **never decides**: it never states an examination outcome, never says how
  confident it is that a ground applies, and never applies the law to an
  application's facts. When asked, it declines and explains the general practice
  instead.
- The examiner's judgement and the Registrar's discretion are untouched.

## 11. Use case 2: applicants, understanding requirements, the process and objections

*This is a possible next use. The demonstrator was built for examiners, so what
follows describes what the same ontology could offer applicants, not a service that
exists today.*

**The goal:** help people applying for a trade mark understand what the requirements
are, how the application process works, and what an objection raised during
examination means.

**Why an ontology suits this audience:**

1. **Applicants use everyday words.** They say "famous person", "brand name",
   "someone else has a similar mark". The ontology's wordings, including everyday
   phrasings, bridge from their language to the Manual's.
2. **An objection becomes a small, readable map.** For a "not capable of
   distinguishing" objection, the map shows the ground (section 41 of the Act), the
   test, what the Manual says can help (evidence of use) and where the how-to guidance
   is (the annex on supplying evidence of use). For a section 43 objection about a
   well-known person, it shows the role of written permission. Section 5 and section 6
   show both maps.
3. **The process becomes a picture.** Roles, steps and documents (application →
   examination → adverse report → response → acceptance → opposition → registration
   and renewal), with what happens at each step and which document it produces.
4. **Answers that show their sources.** Every statement points to the Manual or the
   Act, clearly labelled, so an applicant can read the original for themselves.
5. **The same map, a different door.** The applicant view would reuse the examiners'
   ontology. Both audiences would then read the same practice, described with the same
   ideas.

**The benchmark already contains applicant-style questions**, written in the first
person: "Can a rose variety name also be my trade mark?", "Does using a well-known
person's name in my trade mark raise a refusal…?", "Can I register a brand name that
is almost the same as a protected wine region name, if it's only a slight spelling
change?" The prepared answers show the behaviour an applicant-facing tool would need.
For the wine question the answer said: *"A slight spelling change is not an automatic
way around the objection… I cannot say whether your particular brand would be
registered."*

**Guardrails that matter even more for applicants:**

- It explains what the Manual and the legislation say. It **does not predict whether
  an application will succeed**, and it is not legal advice.
- It keeps practice and law apart, so nobody mistakes office practice for a statutory
  rule.
- It shows which content an expert has reviewed and which a machine drafted.

**Possible benefits** (expected, not measured): applicants arriving better prepared,
fewer avoidable objections, and objections that are easier to understand and answer.

## 12. How the demonstrator was built: machines draft, people judge

The project's principle is **"automation-first, but human-governed"**: machines do the
repetitive drafting, and people make the legal judgements.

1. **A faithful copy of the source, with no AI involved.** A separate tool captured the
   Manual (500 pages, 2,460 passages) and the Act and Regulations (763 provisions)
   deterministically, giving every passage and provision a permanent address. 97% of
   the Manual's references to provisions were matched automatically. Nothing here
   rewrites or "corrects" the source.
2. **Linking ideas to text, with no AI involved.** Matching each idea's wordings against
   the passages produced 7,128 links across 1,649 passages.
3. **One AI drafting pass, in bulk.** A language model proposed connections between
   ideas, definitions for new ideas and everyday phrasings. Software, not the model,
   did the bookkeeping. It found every quoted sentence in the source letter for letter
   and recorded the address and fingerprint. It stamped each record "unreviewed" with
   the model's name and the date, and it refused any reply that failed those checks.
4. **Automatic quality checks.** The finished graph is tested against shape rules
   (SHACL), with **0 defects**. A connection between the wrong kinds of idea is caught.
   All **164 ideas sit in one connected map**, with no islands.
5. **Expert review where it counts.** A trade marks expert reviewed 52 ideas, a set
   of connections between them, ten test questions with the passages each should find,
   and a list of things the system must never do. That reviewed set is kept frozen and separate, as an independent yardstick
   for measuring the machine's work.
6. **Cost.** The recorded spend on AI model calls for the whole demonstrator was about
   **US$8.40**. That covers drafting, answering 129 test questions and grading search
   results. The expensive part of an ontology is not the computing. It is the expert
   time to review and keep it current.

## 13. What has been measured, honestly

The project measured whether the ontology actually improves search, rather than
assuming it.

**The test.** 129 questions: 119 written by an AI model in four kinds (looking
something up, everyday problems in plain words, questions that cross Parts, and "what
does this provision change") plus 10 questions a trade marks expert reviewed. Three
search systems were fixed before any result was graded:

- **keyword**: words only, like a search box;
- **hybrid**: keyword plus matching by meaning. This is good modern search, with no
  ontology;
- **ontology**: hybrid plus recognising ideas, following their connections and adding
  their linked passages.

Another AI model graded every passage in each system's top results for relevance.
Each system's top ten was then scored for quality out of 100 (a standard measure
called nDCG@10). Differences are reported with 95% confidence intervals.

**The results (measured 9 October 2026):**

| | Keyword | Hybrid | Ontology |
|---|---|---|---|
| All 129 questions | 72.5 | 84.4 | 80.3 |
| Everyday problems in plain words (29) | 67.7 | 84.8 | 82.7 |

- **Against keyword search, the ontology clearly helped:** +7.9 points overall (95%
  interval +4.2 to +11.6). The biggest gain was on everyday-language questions: +15.1
  (+8.1 to +22.8).
- **Against good modern search, the ontology ranked slightly worse overall:** −4.1
  points (−6.7 to −1.7). The diagnosis: very common ideas pulled loosely related
  passages into the top ten. *Registrar*, for example, is named in more than 400
  passages.
- **On the expert's 10 questions**, the ontology edged ahead of modern search (+3.0,
  interval +0.8 to +5.5). But ten questions is too few to rely on.

**How to read this.** The ontology's clearest value is **not** "it beats the best
search engine at ranking". It is:

- bridging everyday language to the Manual's terms;
- showing its working: which ideas, which connections, which passages;
- connecting guidance across Parts;
- keeping practice and law apart;
- impact analysis when the text changes; and
- a structure that people, training and other systems can share.

**Caveats.** AI models wrote most of the questions and graded the results; no person
has graded them. The connections were restructured after this measurement, and a fresh
measurement is pending. These numbers describe a prototype, not a finished system.

## 14. Limits and guardrails

**What the demonstrator will never do:**

- Decide an application, state an examination outcome, or say how likely it is that a
  ground applies.
- Present the Manual as law, or let Manual practice and the legislation blur together.
- Present machine-written content as expert-reviewed.

**What is not finished or not yet checked:**

- **Most of it is unreviewed.** 112 of 164 ideas, 352 of 372 connections, every idea's
  kind and all 129 prepared answers were written by a machine, and no trade marks
  expert has reviewed them.
- **Some ideas are recorded twice.** For example, *Registrar* and *Registrar of Trade
  Marks* are one idea under two records. These are flagged for a person to decide, not
  merged automatically.
- **Recognising an idea means matching its wordings**, so an everyday word can collide
  with a legal term: "who has to *sign*" matches the idea *sign*.
- **One snapshot.** The ontology is built over one copy of the Manual. There is no
  automatic update loop yet, although every record is anchored so that one can be
  built.
- **Search ranking:** as measured, it does not beat good modern search overall
  (section 13).

**The most useful thing an examiner can do with the map is find what it has wrong.**

## 15. How to build or adopt an ontology: a recipe

These steps distil what this project did and what it learned.

1. **Start from the authoritative text and freeze a copy.** Give every passage and
   provision a permanent address. Never edit the source to suit the model.
2. **Say what it is for.** Write down the questions it must help answer (called
   *competency questions*) and the things it must never do (*prohibited uses*: here,
   never stating an examination outcome).
3. **List the ideas, with every wording.** Record the formal term, the variants and
   the everyday phrasings. Also record what each idea is *not*.
4. **Sort the ideas into a small number of kinds.** Eleven kinds in three families was
   enough for trade marks examination. Kinds say what an idea is; connections say what
   it does.
5. **Connect them with named relationships.** Never use a vague "related to". Each
   relationship type has a definition and says which kinds it may join.
6. **Pin every statement to its evidence.** Quote the exact sentence, record its
   address, keep a fingerprint.
7. **Label who wrote what.** Keep machine drafts and expert approvals apart. Nothing
   becomes approved by being left alone.
8. **Check it automatically.** Shape rules catch missing evidence, wrong-way
   connections and duplicate names before anything is published.
9. **Put it to work.** Use it for search, question answering, a browsable map, training
   and impact analysis.
10. **Measure honestly.** Compare against the best ordinary search, not just a weak
    one, and report the results that go against you too.
11. **Review and maintain.** Have experts review a sample first, so you get a measured
    error rate. Then re-check exactly what the fingerprints flag as the text changes.

**Lessons learned along the way:**

- **Let machines draft and people judge.** A model can draft hundreds of connections
  for a few dollars. A person's time is best spent judging them.
- **Deterministic first.** Anything that can be done by exact matching should be,
  because it needs no review.
- **Common ideas need care.** An idea named everywhere, such as *Registrar*, can flood
  search results.
- **A co-mention is not a relationship.** Two ideas appearing in the same passage does
  not mean one bears on the other.
- **Keep the yardstick independent.** The expert-reviewed set was frozen so it could
  fairly measure the machine's work.

## 16. Frequently asked questions

**Is this just AI?**
No. The ontology is a structured map that people can read, check and correct. AI
helped draft parts of it, and every AI-drafted part says so. An AI assistant can
*use* the map to find better evidence and show its working, but the map is useful on
its own.

**Does it make decisions about trade marks?**
No. It never states an examination outcome, never predicts whether an application will
be accepted and never says how likely it is that a ground applies. When asked, it
declines and explains the general practice instead.

**Does it replace the Manual?**
No. It points into the Manual and the legislation, which remain the authority. Every
statement it makes cites the exact sentence it rests on.

**Is the Manual the law?**
No. The Manual is the Registrar's practice. The Trade Marks Act 1995 and the Trade
Marks Regulations 1995 are the law. The ontology keeps them visibly apart, including
inside answers.

**Who checked it?**
A trade marks expert reviewed 52 of the 164 ideas, 20 of the 372 connections and ten
test questions. Everything else was drafted by a machine and is labelled "unreviewed".
The next step would be examiners reviewing it, starting with a sample.

**What happens when the Manual changes?**
Every connection stores a fingerprint of the exact text it quotes. When a page
changes, the system lists exactly which connections need re-checking and leaves the
rest alone.

**How is this different from a search engine?**
A search engine matches words. The ontology knows that different words can name the
same idea, that one word can name different ideas, how ideas affect one another, and
where each of those facts is written down. It can also show *why* a passage was found.

**Why not just ask a chatbot?**
A chatbot on its own can sound confident without showing where its answer came from.
Grounding it in the ontology means the answer is built only from real passages,
follows visible connections, keeps practice and law apart, and has every quote checked
word for word by a computer.

**What did it cost?**
About US$8.40 in AI model calls for the whole demonstrator. The real cost of an
ontology is expert time to review it and keep it current.

**Could applicants use it?**
The same ontology could power plain-language guidance for applicants: what the
requirements are, how the process works and what an objection means. It would always
show its sources and never predict an outcome. This has not been built yet.

## 17. Glossary

- **Ontology:** a shared, explicit description of a field: its ideas, what kind of
  thing each is, and how they relate.
- **Knowledge graph:** the actual content held in the shape the ontology describes: the
  specific ideas, connections, passages and provisions.
- **Idea / concept:** one thing people reason about, whatever words are used for it.
- **Label / wording:** a word or phrase used for an idea. One is preferred; others are
  alternatives. A "not" label is a lookalike wording that means something else.
- **Kind:** what sort of thing an idea is, such as a ground of refusal, a legal test or
  a procedural step.
- **Connection / relationship:** a named link saying how one idea bears on another,
  such as "is overcome by" or "may give rise to".
- **Triple:** one statement in three parts: subject, connection, object.
- **Evidence / source span:** the exact quoted sentence a statement rests on, with its
  address.
- **Fingerprint (content hash):** a short code computed from exact text. It changes if
  the text changes.
- **Unreviewed:** written by a machine and not yet checked by a person.
- **Reviewed / approved:** checked and accepted by a named expert on a recorded date.
- **Impact analysis:** working out what is affected when a passage or provision
  changes.
- **The Manual:** the Trade Marks Manual of Practice and Procedure, the Registrar's
  examination practice. Not law.
- **The Act:** the Trade Marks Act 1995. **The Regulations:** the Trade Marks
  Regulations 1995. Together, the law.
- **Ground for rejection / ground of refusal:** a reason in the Act for which an
  application can be refused, such as section 41 (not capable of distinguishing) or
  section 43 (likely to deceive or cause confusion).
- **Adverse report:** the examiner's written report of problems with an application,
  including any grounds for rejection.
- **Specification of goods and services:** the list of goods and services an
  application covers.
- **SKOS, OWL, RDF, SHACL:** open W3C standards for, respectively, vocabularies,
  ontologies, storing statements as triples and checking data quality.
- **nDCG@10:** a standard score (here out of 100) for how good a search system's top
  ten results are.

## 19. A suggested storyboard

An optional running order for an explainer of about six to eight minutes.

1. **The hook.** Someone asks: "Can I use a famous person's name in my trade mark?" The
   answer is in the Manual, somewhere in 2,460 passages across 54 Parts, beside the Act
   and the Regulations.
2. **Words are not ideas.** The specification example: 3 passages use the full formal
   term, but 109 name the idea. Then the flip side: "consent" and "sign" each mean
   more than one thing.
3. **Book of directions vs map.** Introduce the ontology with the city-map analogy and
   its ingredients: ideas, wordings, kinds, connections, receipts and who wrote it.
4. **Draw a little map.** Worked example A (the well-known person), arrow by arrow, each
   arrow opening onto its sentence.
5. **Zoom out.** Three families and eleven kinds. *What a mark contains and its context
   bear on the tests; the tests decide the grounds.*
6. **Put it to work for examiners.** Ask the Manual: recognise, follow, gather, answer.
   The working is shown, practice and law are kept apart, and it never decides.
7. **The same map for applicants.** Explaining an objection (worked example B) and the
   process (worked example C), described as a possible next step.
8. **When the text changes.** Fingerprints and impact analysis.
9. **Honesty.** Who wrote what; what was measured, in both directions; the limits.
10. **How to build one.** The recipe in one breath. Close: *"The Manual tells you what.
    The ontology shows how it all connects, and where it says so."*

## 20. Notes for the presenters

**Tone.** Friendly, concrete and curious, like a good teacher. Explain every technical
term the first time it is used, with an everyday comparison. Prefer the worked
examples in this document to abstract description.

**Words and pronunciation.**

- *Ontology*: on-TOL-uh-jee.
- *Trade mark* is two words in Australia. Use Australian spelling: recognise, organise,
  licence (the noun).
- Say "the Manual" for the Trade Marks Manual of Practice and Procedure, "the Act" for
  the Trade Marks Act 1995 and "the Regulations" for the Trade Marks Regulations 1995.
- Say "section 41" and "section 43" in full rather than "s 41".
- *SHACL*, if mentioned at all, is said "shackle". *nDCG* need not be said; "a score
  out of 100 for the quality of the top ten results" is enough.

**Always:**

- Say that the tool supports people's judgement and never makes the decision.
- Keep "the Manual is practice; the Act and Regulations are law" visible.
- Say that most of the content was drafted by a machine and is labelled unreviewed.
- When showing a connection, say it comes with the sentence it rests on.
- If the measurement is mentioned, give both directions: better than keyword search,
  especially for everyday language; slightly behind good modern search on ranking
  overall.

**Never:**

- Say or imply that the system approves, rejects, accepts or refuses applications, or
  predicts whether a particular trade mark will be registered.
- Call the ontology law, official guidance or legal advice. Do not call it an IP
  Australia service: it is a demonstrator built over IP Australia's published Manual.
- Say the AI "understands the law" or that the content has been checked by experts,
  beyond the 52 ideas and 20 connections that have been reviewed.
- Name any individual, or use any government logo or crest.
- Invent trade mark examples, case outcomes or statistics not in this document. The
  example marks quoted from the Manual (FORGED IN THE OUTBACK for vehicles, ANTARCTIC
  BERRIES for frozen strawberries, MOCHA for confectionery, from Part 29's discussion
  of geographical references) may be used as quoted.

**Visual ideas.**

- The Manual as a tall stack of pages, each passage a small square: 2,460 of them, in
  54 coloured bands for the Parts.
- The same squares lighting up when an idea is found under different wordings.
- Ideas as labelled circles, coloured by kind; connections as arrows labelled with
  their verb. Clicking an arrow opens a quote card.
- Manual passages as **blue squares**, Act provisions as **amber diamonds** and
  Regulations as **plum hexagons**, as in the demonstrator.
- An orange "Machine · unreviewed" tag on machine-drafted items; a plain grey
  "Reviewed" on expert-reviewed ones.
- Zooming out from ideas to three families of kinds, and from there to one sentence:
  *what a mark contains and its context bear on the tests; the tests decide the
  grounds.*
- A page being edited, with only the arrows that rest on it flashing for re-checking.
